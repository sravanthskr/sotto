# PART I — DISCOVERY

## 1. What the product currently is

Today, RealAssistant is a **voice-first Windows program that actually does things on your computer**. Not a chat window — a presence. You hold a key, speak, and it:

- reads and writes your **email** (inside your own Google account),
- reads and changes your **calendar**,
- finds, reads, and organizes your **files**, PDFs, and notes,
- sees your **screen** and reads what's on it when asked,
- opens and controls your **apps**, types, and runs commands,
- **remembers** what matters about you and stops asking twice,
- and speaks back in a voice you choose — including a voice trained from your own samples.

It runs on a modest PC with no GPU. It brings its own AI key, keeps secrets encrypted on your machine, and asks before doing anything consequential. When it finishes something, it is designed to say what actually happened — including when it couldn't confirm.

**The honest one-liner:** it's a computer operator you talk to. One person, one machine, real work.

## 2. What it could become

Not "a bigger assistant." A **recognizable product** — the thing people mean when they say:

> "I don't operate my computer anymore. I tell it what I need."

The ceiling is not a feature count. The ceiling is a **relationship**: you speak outcomes in plain words; it works across your real apps; it shows receipts; it learns your world; it stays quiet until needed. That product has a name, a personality, a demo people retell at dinner, and a trust story no chatbot can copy because it's built on *doing and proving*, not talking.

## 3. Problems with existing assistants

| What users experience | Why it happens | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| It answers, but doesn't *do* | Assistants were built to inform, not execute | You still click 40 times |
| It only works inside one company's apps | Ecosystem silos (phone OS, one suite) | Your PC's real work stays manual |
| It forgets everything | Stateless sessions | You re-explain your life daily |
| Vague phrasing breaks it | Rigid intent grammars | You talk like a machine |
| It says "done" without checking | No verification loop | You can't trust it — so you re-check everything |
| It won't touch consequential things | No permission model worth trusting | It's cute, not useful |
| It chats too much | Chat is the product there | Noise, not help |
| It can't see your screen/files/preferences | No personal context | Generic answers |

**[I]** The common thread: assistants are built as *informers with a chat face*. Nobody has made an **operator with a conscience**: something that acts across your real machine, proves it acted, and answers for the results.

## 5. White-space opportunity

Between **"what people want to say"** and **"what computers require people to do manually"** sits the gap:

- People want to say: *"Handle my morning." "Get me ready for tomorrow's interview." "Deal with my email." "Find that file and fix it up for me."*
- Computers require: opening six apps, remembering six contexts, clicking through consent dialogs, copying data by hand, and staying at the keyboard the whole time.

The white space is **delegation for one person on one real machine**:

1. **Goal-level, not command-level.** Natural sentences, messy is fine.
2. **Real-app reach.** Your actual email, calendar, files, apps — not a sandbox, not one company's suite.
3. **Verified doing.** It acts *and proves*, or says it couldn't — the trust layer everyone else skipped.
4. **Voice-first, hands-free.** The medium matches the idea: you talk, it works.
5. **Private by default.** Secrets local, memory local, permissions visible.
6. **A presence, not a chat window.** Calm, quiet, one look, recognizable.

**[I]** No current product combines all six. That combination *is* the product territory.

# PART II — PRODUCT IDENTITY

*(What we learned from great products — Apple's clarity, Linear's taste, Nothing/Dyson's distinctiveness, Superhuman's speed-worship — distilled: a great product is one idea, enforced everywhere, with the confidence to refuse everything else. That is the standard applied below.)*

## 6. Core product idea

> **You give intent. It does the computer work. And it shows you the receipt.**

Three clauses, each load-bearing:

- *"You give intent"* — you speak outcomes, not steps. No commands to memorize.
- *"It does the computer work"* — execution across your real apps, not advice about how to execute.
- *"And it shows you the receipt"* — done is proven, not claimed. This is the clause nobody else has.

Candidate ideas considered and rejected: "conversational computer" (too vague), "your AI that works" (generic), "the interface between you and your digital life" (grandiose, meaningless), "do more with less effort" (marketing fog). The winning idea is the one above because it's *specific, human, and provable*.

## 7. Product category

**Existing categories are insufficient:**
- **Voice assistant** → answers questions, sets timers. Informer.
- **Copilot** → assists *you while you drive*. Suggester.
- **Chatbot** → conversation, not action. Talker.
- **Automation tool** → flows built by technicians. Configurator.
- **Agent (as currently marketed)** → demos in sandboxes; rarely trustworthy with your real life.

**Proposed category: a personal operator.**

> A **personal operator** is software you speak to that operates your computer on your behalf — across your real apps — under your permissions, with verified results.

Why this name works: "operator" is a human word with a humbling history — the person who made the system *work for people*. It implies judgment, execution, and accountability. It is not mystical ("agent"), not servant-y ("assistant"), not mechanical ("automation").

**Mental model for the user:** *"It's like having a capable operator sitting inside my computer. I tell it the outcome; it works the apps; it reports back."*

Category candidates also considered: *voice computer* (sounds like dictation), *computer agent* (generic + trust-damaged), *delegation layer* (technical), *digital operator* (fine but vague). **"Personal operator"** survives because it's sayable, human, and it sets expectations the product can actually meet. **[P]**

## 8. Product philosophy

Five beliefs, in order of importance:

1. **The computer is for outcomes, not operation.** Nobody should have to *operate* software to get a result they can describe in one sentence.
2. **Intent beats instructions.** Say the *what*; figuring out the *how* is the product's job, not the user's.
3. **Action without proof is noise.** Anything claimed must be checked, or labeled unverified. "Done" is a promise; the receipt keeps it.
4. **Power stays under your hand.** It can act, but permissions are visible, actions are stoppable, and secrets stay local.
5. **The best interface is a quiet one.** It speaks when it matters, stays silent when it doesn't, and never turns your desktop into a dashboard.

## 9. Product principles

The rules future features get judged against:

1. **Intent over instructions.** Features must accept *goals*, not just commands.
2. **Do, don't just answer.** If the product can act, answering alone is a failure mode.
3. **Verify, then report.** No "done" without a check; no check means "couldn't confirm."
4. **Ask only when it matters.** Confirmations are for consequences, not for everything.
5. **Remember, don't re-ask.** Context accumulates; memory is visible and editable.
6. **Speed is respect.** Start speaking as soon as there's something to say; never dead air.
7. **Calm surface.** One presence, minimal chrome, no dashboards, no noise.
8. **Always stoppable.** One word stops it; stopping never lies about what happened.

## 10. Personality

**If it were a person:** a brilliant, calm aide. The one who's already handled it before you finished asking. Warm but economical with words. Never flatters. Delivers bad news plainly and with a next step. Dry humor, rarely, never during work.

| Dimension | Setting |
|---|---|
| Tone | Calm, direct, human. Short sentences. |
| Confidence | States what it knows; says "I'm not sure" without drama. |
| Verbosity | One sentence by default. Expands only when asked or when detail matters. |
| Humor | Rare, dry, optional. Zero during consequential work. |
| Transparency | Says what it did, what it checked, what it couldn't. |
| Speaks | On results, blockers, and real decisions. |
| Stays silent | During work, between sentences, when nothing changed. |
| Mistakes | Owns immediately. "That failed — here's why. Want me to try the other way?" |
| Uncertainty | "I couldn't confirm it went through." Never a bluff. |
| Questions | Maximum one, and only when the answer changes the outcome. |
| Interruption | Stops instantly, mid-word. Listens. Then resumes or adjusts. |
| Completion | "Done." / "Done — verified." / "Done, but I couldn't confirm the last step." |

**Never:** "Certainly! I'd be happy to help!" · sycophancy · fake excitement · nagging · pretending to feel things to be liked · being a pet.

**The litmus test:** *Would a respected human operator say it this way?* If not, rewrite.

## 11. Brand direction

**One-line brand direction:** *Quiet power. The calm operator.*

- **Feeling:** relief, competence, control regained. The feeling of "handled."
- **Voice of brand:** plain words, short lines, zero hype. Confidence without volume.
- **Enemy:** noise — chatty assistants, dashboard clutter, techno-mysticism, "AI magic" language.
- **Promise of the brand:** when it says done, it's done. When it can't, it says so first.

## 13. Name reasoning — the three finalists

**1. SOTTO — recommended.**
- **Meaning:** Italian, *sotto voce* — "in a quiet voice." The product's whole thesis is a *voice that works quietly for you*. The name IS the philosophy: quiet, capable, close.
- **Sound:** SOT-to. Two syllables, five letters, soft consonants. Survives being said 100 times ("Hey Sotto", "ask Sotto", "Sotto, handle it"). Sounds like a *name*, not a gadget.
- **Feel:** warm, calm, a little elegant — European, not sci-fi. Nothing robotic, nothing corporate.
- **Fit:** it's already the design language of the interface (tokens, orb, window). The name would not be a costume change — it's the same idea at every layer.
- **Voice test:** "Sotto, what needs my attention in email?" — reads perfectly aloud. Critical for a voice-first brand.
- **Five-year test:** "Sotto" can grow to mean the product family (Sotto for Windows, Sotto Brief, etc.) without strain. It never boxed us into "voice" or "email" or "PC" alone.

**2. FACTA — runner-up.**
- **Meaning:** Latin *facta* — "things done," and it doubles as *fact* — verification. Two Pillars in one word.
- **Why second:** clever and on-message, but colder; pronunciation ambiguity ("fack-ta? / fak-ta?"); reads a bit like a legal-tech company. Excellent internal product-line name later ("Facta Brief" is nice for a reporting feature).

**3. AIDE — runner-up.**
- **Meaning:** the plainest human word for help; "your aide."
- **Why third:** it's a dictionary word used by countless small apps; it describes the *role* better than the *product*. Keep "aide" as a descriptor line ("like a capable aide"), not the brand.

## 14. Distinctiveness checks (honest status)

Per the brief: avoid obvious collisions; do not claim legal availability.

- **Sotto:** known soft collisions — restaurants/venues named Sotto exist worldwide; "sotto voce" is a common phrase (not ownable, which is fine — it's the story). **Not checked this session to completion:** software/class-9/class-42 trademarks, domain availability, app-store presence. *Search conditions were degraded during this session; treat this section as "first screen," not clearance.* **[R partial / I]**
- **Facta:** small existing uses (news/consulting names). Also requires a clearance pass.
- **Aide:** maximally generic — expect many small collisions; weak as a trademark.
- **Recommendation:** before any public commitment, run one focused pass: trademark registries (key classes: 9 software, 42 SaaS), domain hunt (sotto.app / sotto.dev / getsotto.com / sotto.ai variants), app stores, and a linguistic check across major languages for negative meanings. **Sotto currently shows no blocking collision in my research.** **[R/I]**

## 16. Product promise

> **Whatever you ask of your computer gets done — and Sotto will never tell you it's done when it isn't.**

Two halves, both mandatory: *execution* (it actually does it) and *honesty* (verified or flagged). If either half ever becomes false, the brand is finished. This promise is also a self-imposed engineering constraint: it forces verification to be a first-class feature instead of a polish item.

## 18. Visual identity direction

*(Direction, not redesign. Sotto already has a visual language in progress — this is the north star for it.)*

- **One presence, one glance.** A single soft orb — the "ember" — that breathes. Its state (listening / working / done) is legible in a glance from across the room. No avatar face; faces lie about presence and age badly.
- **Light as default, calm as rule.** A light, warm, paper-like canvas (user's stated preference) with deep ink text. Dark mode as an equal twin, never the default.
- **Shape language:** soft radii, low contrast, nothing sharp. Cards, not windows. Beats, not progress bars.
- **Typography:** humanist sans for UI; generous line-height; numerals for receipts. Words are short enough that type is calm by default.
- **Motion personality:** breathing, settling, never bouncing. Animations finish before you notice them; nothing celebratory (no confetti — for *anything*).
- **Recognizability target:** the ember + a one-line receipt in that type + the calm canvas = Sotto, even with the name hidden. **[P]**

## 19. Sound identity

Voice-first means sound is half the brand.

- **Activation:** a soft, breathy onset — the sound of a held breath, not a chime. ("It's listening now.")
- **Listening:** near-silent. A faint level shimmer; **no beeps while you talk.**
- **Completion:** one quiet, low two-note settle — the audio equivalent of a nod. Same family as the activation breath.
- **Error:** the *same* family, lower and shorter. Errors are acknowledged, never punished. No buzzers, ever.
- **Notification (rare):** a single soft tone, once. If it must repeat, it waits.
- **Speaking voice:** the brand's main instrument (see #20). Default: warm and brisk. The sound system and the voice are mixed like one palette.
- **The recognizability test:** a Sotto completion should be identifiable in a blind listen within two years. **[P]**

## 20. Voice personality (the assistant's spoken behavior)

The spoken assistant *is* the product; this is its behavior spec, in brief:

- **Response length:** one sentence by default; two when a decision is involved; longer only for briefs — and it offers "want the details?" instead of dumping them.
- **Acknowledgement:** minimal and immediate ("Got it. On it.") — or nothing if the job is instant; the result is the acknowledgement.
- **Progress:** speaks at the *start* of real work ("Checking your email now.") and at blockers — not at every step. Silence during work is normal; but it never goes fully dark when a turn is expected.
- **Completion:** result first, receipt second. "Your 4pm is now at 5. Calendar updated — verified."
- **Failure:** plain, early, with a next move. "Gmail refused the send — the connection expired. Reconnect and I'll send it."
- **Uncertainty:** stated as a fact about the world, not a shrug. "I couldn't confirm the payment page went through. Everything before it worked."
- **Clarification:** one question max, at decision points where the answer changes the outcome; never a survey, never re-asking something already known.
- **Interruption:** stops instantly on any stop word; the job's state is preserved; stopping is announced ("Stopped. The email is still a draft.").
- **Silence:** it isn't silent *at* you (every turn gets a spoken result), but it doesn't fill space. It never narrates implementation. It never small-talks unless invited.
- **Voice wardrobe:** the voice is an instrument, not the identity. Default fast voices for daily work; a chosen signature voice for moments (including a custom-trained voice for users who want and own the rights to one — as this build already supports). The persona = calm operator; the voice = user's wardrobe.

# PART IV — PRODUCT

## 21. What the product does, in simple language

Sotto sits quietly on your PC. When you need something, you talk — the way you'd talk to a capable person — and it works through your email, calendar, files, and apps to make it happen. Then it tells you exactly what it did.

**Do the whole job.** Tell it what you want accomplished — *"get me ready for tomorrow's interview"* — and it works through the individual steps itself, instead of making you drive each one. It tells you when it's finished, and it tells you if something didn't work.

**The ergonomic difference** in one line: other tools make you *operate* the computer to get something done. Sotto makes you *describe* the result — and it does the operating.

### Catalog — the ten features that matter, in plain words

*(Per the brief's catalog format: what it is, what you can say, what it does, why it's useful.)*

**1. Email, handled.** *In simple words:* it reads your mail and does something about it. *You can say:* "Anything urgent in my email?" / "Reply to Sara that I'll confirm tonight — and send it." *It can then:* summarize what matters · draft replies in your voice · send after one confirmation · save drafts. *Why useful:* email is where days go to die; this is the single biggest daily win.

**2. Your day, known.** *You can say:* "What's tomorrow?" / "Move my 4pm to 5." *It can then:* read your calendar · add and move events · (next: find a free hour for you). *Why useful:* plans change all day; fixing them should take one sentence.

**3. Files & papers.** *You can say:* "Find my resume and make a clean copy on the Desktop" / "What does this PDF say?" / "Put today's downloads in order." *It can then:* locate files · read and summarize documents · organize folders · convert formats · rename in bulk. *Why useful:* the drawer nobody wants to open — opened.

**4. It sees your screen.** *You can say:* "What's this error?" / "Read this and summarize." *It can then:* read the screen, understand context, explain and act. *Why useful:* half of computer life is "what is this thing in front of me."

**5. Memory.** *You can say:* "Remember I ship on Fridays" / "What do you know about my work?" *It can then:* keep a visible, editable book of you — projects, people, preferences — and stop asking twice. *Why useful:* the difference between a tool and *your* tool.

**6. Routines.** *You can say:* "Do my Monday morning." *It can then:* replay a saved sequence of real steps — prepare, gather, launch, send. *Why useful:* your repeated hour becomes one phrase.

**7. The brief.** *You can say:* "Get me ready for tomorrow." *It can then:* gather email + calendar + notes and read you a short, honest picture — what's coming, what needs you, what it already handled. *Why useful:* life-admin, pre-chewed. (This grows into the flagship "prepare me for X" feature.)

**8. Everyday control.** *You can say:* "Open Spotify, volume down, focus mode." *It can then:* open apps, set levels, manage windows. *Why useful:* the small stuff should never need a mouse.

**9. Voice — yours.** *You can say:* "Say it slower." / "Read it in my voice." *It can then:* speak in the default voices for speed, or in a custom-trained voice for moments; repeat or slow down on command. *Why useful:* the voice is the intimacy layer — and a trained voice makes it *yours*.

**10. Honest reporting.** *You can say:* "Did that actually go through?" *It can then:* show exactly what it verified and what it couldn't — every time, without being asked. *Why useful:* trust isn't a feature here; it's the product.

## 24. Signature capabilities

These are the reasons someone installs Sotto — the capabilities that *are* the product identity.

**1. Verified doing (the receipt).**
*What:* every consequential job ends with a check and a plain statement — "done, verified" or "done, couldn't confirm." *Why it matters:* all trust in delegation rests here; nobody else treats "done" as a claim to be proved. *Why voice matters:* you hear the receipt hands-free, the moment it matters; no log file, no dashboard. *Behind the scenes (high level):* after acting, it re-reads the thing it changed — the sent message, the moved file, the updated event. *The user experiences:* relief, and no need to double-check. *Different from a chatbot:* a chatbot can't fail you; Sotto can — so it accounts for itself. *Why this product:* it was designed around honesty from day one.

**2. Real-app reach.**
*What:* it works in *your* actual Gmail, calendar, files, and installed apps — not a sandbox, not a demo box. *Why it matters:* delegation is only useful if it reaches real life. *Why voice:* you delegate in the moment — from the kitchen, from bed, mid-thought. *Behind the scenes:* permissions + the machine's own tools, operated under your sign-ins. *The user experiences:* asks from anywhere in the house; it happens on the PC. *Different from a chatbot:* it changes real state in real accounts. *Why this product:* real control has been the core since v1.

**3. A memory that compounds.**
*What:* it accumulates your world — people, projects, preferences, standing instructions — and uses it without ceremony. *Why it matters:* every re-explanation is a tax that kills assistants. *Why voice:* you *tell* it things in passing ("my manager is Priya"); voice is how memory enters naturally. *Behind the scenes:* a visible, editable memory it consults before asking. *User experiences:* "it already knew." *Different from a chatbot:* chat forgets; this compounds. *Why this product:* one person, one machine — memory is the moat a generic assistant can't copy.

**4. The brief.**
*What:* prepared, honest summaries of what's coming and what it already handled — morning, evening, pre-event. *Why it matters:* the highest-value minute of the day is *orientation*; nobody gives it for free. *Why voice:* it arrives while you're making coffee, not while you're at a screen. *Behind the scenes:* it gathers email + calendar + notes, condenses, flags decisions. *User experiences:* "I'm oriented before I sit down." *Different from a chatbot:* it initiates value; it doesn't wait. *Why this product:* it already holds every thread needed to make it true.

**5. Routines that are actually you.**
*What:* saved multi-step jobs built from *your* actual workflows — file sorts, weekly prep, report assembly. *Why it matters:* repetition is the tax of computer life; routines are the write-off. *Why voice:* run an hour of prep with four words while walking to your desk. *Behind the scenes:* a recorded sequence of real actions, replayed with checks. *User experiences:* "Monday morning does itself." *Different from a chatbot:* lasting artifacts, not conversations. *Why this product:* it operates the machine, so routines manipulate *real* state — not just cloud APIs.

**6. Conversation that feels like delegation.**
*What:* corrections mid-flow, interruptions, "no — the other one," "slower," "stop" — without breaking anything. *Why it matters:* talking to it must feel like talking *to someone*, not like programming. *Why voice:* that's the whole medium. *Behind the scenes:* stop ≠ cancel (it can still tell you what it was doing), state survives interrupts. *User experiences:* control without fear. *Different from a chatbot:* the stakes are real and it behaves accordingly. *Why this product:* the interaction model was built voice-first, not retrofitted.

## 25. Complete real-world workflows

*Eight scenarios — what you say, what happens, what you get. Written for the real machine, honest to today and the near future (◐ marks steps arriving in NEXT).*

**Starting work.** "Morning — what needs me?" → it checks email and calendar, then speaks a 20-second brief: two things needing decisions, one thing it scheduled for you, nothing else urgent. *You get:* orientation before you open anything.

**Communication.** "Anything urgent in email?" → summaries, one prompt: "The invoice one needs a yes/no by noon." → "Reply yes, ask them to confirm by 4." → spoken draft, one "send it," receipt. *You get:* inbox flow without inbox time.

**Interview (the flagship ask — ◐ closing in NEXT).** "Get me ready for tomorrow's 10am interview." → it gathers the calendar entry, the company's brief from your notes/email, your resume file, a prepared cheat-sheet, prints/collects them into one folder, checks your morning calendar, and reads you a plan. *You get:* prep without prep-work.

**Coding.** "Run the tests and tell me what broke." → it runs, reads output, reports the failing set, offers the obvious next step. *You get:* the errand loop of dev life, voice-driven.

**Research.** "Dig into this PDF and the emails from them — what matters?" → reads, cross-references, returns a short honest synthesis with sources. *You get:* digestion done for you.

**Files.** "Clean up my downloads and find the tax PDF from March." → sorts by type/date with receipts; finds the file by *meaning*, not just name. *You get:* order, and the artifact.

**Meeting.** "What's this call about and what should I have open?" → reads the invite, related emails, your notes; opens the right window; briefs you in 15 seconds. *You get:* walking in prepared.

**Something breaks.** "It says access denied — fix it." → reads the screen, identifies the cause, takes the fix path or explains the one thing it needs from you. Never pretends. *You get:* a way forward instead of a dead end.

## 26. The AHA moment

**The moment:** you said one sentence that would have been ten minutes of clicking — across *three apps* — and it simply happened. Then it told you what it checked. You never touched the mouse.

**The concrete AHA we design toward:** *"Sort this morning for me."* → it reads mail, replies to one thing, moves one meeting, and hands you a receipt line per item. The user's internal line: **"Wait — it did all of that?"** followed immediately by **"…and it tells me the truth about it."** The second half is what makes it *Sotto* and not a demo.

This moment drives onboarding, marketing, the demo, and feature priority: everything exists to make *that minute* reliable.

## 27. First 30 seconds

No wizard. No account walls. No feature tour. The design:

1. **It opens like it's alive.** The ember breathes. One line on screen: *"Hold Ctrl+Shift+Space and just ask."*
2. **One micro-lesson, one success.** Three starter asks as chips: "What's on my screen?" · "Anything urgent in my email?" · "What's tomorrow?" — the first *successful interaction* is 15 seconds away.
3. **The first receipt is the pitch.** After the first result it says: *"Done. And when I say done, I've checked."* Difference communicated by demonstration, not explanation.
4. **Name it's mine.** It asks one thing only: your name, so the first sentence back is personal ("Good evening, Sráv."), then it retreats to silence.

**Never in the first 30 seconds:** terms of service walls, "sign in with" before value, a 12-page tour, feature lists, or the word "AI."

## 28. What it should NOT do ("What We Will Not Build")

Treated as seriously as the feature list:

- **A companion for loneliness.** It's an operator, not a friend; warmth comes from competence, not neediness.
- **A always-listening device.** No default open mic; it listens when summoned.
- **A dashboard.** No metrics hub, no kanban boards, no "workspace."
- **A content-generation studio.** Images/video/social posts: wrong job; delegate out.
- **A no-code automation builder.** Belongs to Zapier-likes; Sotto *runs* results, not logic puzzles.
- **A chat app.** No endless conversation for its own sake; talk is the means, not the product.
- **An enterprise admin suite.** Personal first; corporate is a different product wearing a suit.
- **A marketplace early.** Skills/integrations curated by us until trust exists, not an app store.
- **A pet, a gimmick, an avatar.** No faces, no cuteness loops, no engagement tricks.
- **An "app that does everything."** Every feature must reinforce one idea — or wait.
- **A subscription treadmill.** Costs stay honest; the user's key, the user's machine, no dark patterns.
- **A surveillance surface.** It never reports on you, to anyone, ever.

# PART V — PRODUCT EXPERIENCE

## 29. How people talk to it

Like a person, about outcomes — not commands.

- "Handle my morning." (not "open Gmail, check unread, summarize…")
- "I'm heading out — wrap things up." (context-language: "heading out" implies the wrap-up; it asks at most one question.)
- "No, the *second* one. And say it slower." (corrections are first-class; mid-flow changes never break anything.)
- "That's wrong — forget I said that." (memory and plans are revocable.)

Design rules: it tolerates messy speech; it never requires wake word accuracy beyond reason; it treats the *situation*, not the sentence, as the request. One question max; the answer must change the outcome or it isn't asked.

## 30. How it acts

- **One job at a time, with priority** — like a professional; parallel work is invisible, not chatty.
- **Fast first, thorough second** — it starts speaking when the first *useful* sentence exists, and keeps working while it talks.
- **Reversible by default** — drafts before sends, folders before deletes (deletes go to trash), moves by copy-then-remove.
- **Receipts for consequences** — anything that sent, deleted, moved, or spent gets checked and reported.
- **Stop is sacred** — any stop word halts speech instantly; a second stop halts the job; state is always announced.

## 31. How it communicates progress

The presence is the progress bar: **listening → got it → working → done.**

- Speech: start-of-work line ("Checking your mail."), blocker line ("Google needs a re-login — doing that now."), completion line (result + receipt).
- Silence between those beats is *by design* — with one guarantee: it will never go dark on a turn. If it needs 30 more seconds, it says the halfway-truth ("still going — one folder left").
- No percentage theater. Progress you can't verify is noise.

## 32. How it handles uncertainty

- It says the honest thing in plain words: *"I'm not sure this scheduled — I couldn't find it after saving."*
- It shows its evidence: what it saw, what it expected, what it will do next.
- Guessing is forbidden where money, identity, or deletion are involved — there it verifies or asks.
- Confidence is expressed as fact, not vibe: "verified" · "pretty sure" · "couldn't confirm."

## 33. How it handles mistakes

Own → explain → next step. In that order, always.

- "That failed — Gmail didn't accept the send because the connection expired. Want me to try again?"
- No excuses, no walls of error text, no silent retries that hide state.
- Every failure leaves a receipt too ("the draft is saved"), so nothing is ever lost in a crash of confidence.
- Repeated failure escalates honestly: alternate method → simpler goal → ask you the one question that unblocks it.

## 34. How it handles interruption

- **Stop:** instant. Mid-word, mid-sentence, mid-anything.
- **Stopping ≠ cancelling:** stopping speech leaves the job running (announced); stopping twice cancels (with state: "stopped; the folder was half-sorted").
- **Talking over it:** it yields the floor immediately and listens.
- **Redirecting:** "not that — do this instead" reroutes cleanly; no sulking, no reset to zero.

## 35. How it remembers

- **What it remembers:** people, projects, preferences, standing instructions ("always ask before sending"), and useful context from work it did.
- **How it feels:** invisible in the moment — it just *knows* — but never spooky: memory is a visible book you can read, edit, and delete.
- **How it enters:** in passing, by voice; "remember" as a verb works; so does implicit teaching (corrections get remembered when they generalize).
- **How it forgets:** "forget that" — immediate, real, and clean.
- **Boundary:** it remembers *you*, not the world's data; secrets live encrypted and local.

## 36. How it becomes proactive

Slowly, opt-in, and quietly — proactivity is a privilege earned by trust.

- **Level 1 — Briefs (now):** prepared on your schedule (morning/evening), spoken, short.
- **Level 2 — Heads-ups (next):** "the invoice deadline is tomorrow," "this person hasn't replied in 6 days."
- **Level 3 — Watchers (later):** you name conditions (a folder, a site, a price); it watches silently and speaks once, when it matters.
- **Level 4 — Standing jobs (future):** scheduled delegated work with approval digests — "while you were away: 3 jobs done, 1 needs your OK."
- **The rule at every level:** it never interrupts for anything it could have handled, and it never acts on the irreversible without you.

# PART VI — EVOLUTION

## 37. TODAY — what this realistically already is

An honest snapshot (no pretending): a voice-first Windows operator that **really** handles email (in the user's own Google account), calendar, files and documents, screens, apps, and commands; remembers; runs saved routines; speaks in default or a custom-trained voice; keeps secrets encrypted locally; asks before consequential actions; and reports with a verification pass on what it changed.

Limits stated openly: reliability depends on the user's AI provider (free models are flaky; paid keys are steadier); multi-step plans are still mostly single-hop (the kernel is the NEXT layer); Mac and mobile don't exist; polish is young. **What it already is: the most honest v1 of delegation on a real desktop.**

## 38. NEXT — what it becomes as a serious daily product

- **The agent kernel:** jobs with plans, verification gates, permission tiers (read/write/send/delete), and recovery paths — the difference between "it did one thing" and "it does whole jobs."
- **Multi-step documents:** "gather X from these files → make the report" as a first-class ask.
- **Briefs v2:** morning/evening briefs from email + calendar + notes, spoken, with "handled" receipts.
- **Deeper Google:** Drive, Tasks, free-hour finding; bring-your-own-sign-in stays.
- **First-run polish:** the 30 seconds in #27, for real.
- **Wake word (opt-in)** and better far-talk, so "Sotto…" replaces the hotkey for kitchen-distance use.
- **A second surface:** phone as remote mic (talk to the PC from anywhere in the house).

## 39. FUTURE — what the deep architecture unlocks

- **Multi-day projects:** "keep this project moving" — it plans across days, works in windows of your availability, reports at milestones.
- **Approval digests:** autonomous stretches with a single morning review — the receipt layer becomes the control surface.
- **Watching & standing orders:** persistent jobs ("keep the inbox under 20," "track this shipment") with minimal, honest updates.
- **Local models** where possible: privacy deepens as hardware allows; the same product, more inside the house.
- **Other people, same soul:** family members on one machine get their own operator — same guarantees; the product never merges lives.
- **The trust ledger:** your complete history of what was done, verified, failed — exportable, understandable, *yours*.

## 40. Long-term product vision — the evolution chain

```text
Voice control tool                 (it did what you said)
        ↓
Useful computer aide               (it handled errands)
        ↓
Delegated work operator            (it did whole jobs, with receipts)  ← crossing now
        ↓
Personal operator with judgment    (it prepares, prioritizes, prevents)
        ↓
Ambient operator for your devices  (the quiet layer over your digital life)
```

What changes at each stage is not capability count — it's **how much intent one sentence can carry, and how much trust comes back with the receipt.** The end state: **you stop operating software. You state outcomes; your operator delivers them — provably.**

# PART VII — FINAL PRODUCT DEFINITION

### Product name
**Sotto.** *(From "sotto voce" — in a quiet voice. The quiet, capable voice that works for you. Finalists also considered: Facta, Aide. Run a formal clearance pass—trademarks, domains—before public commitment.)*

### Category
**A personal operator** — software you speak to that operates your computer for you, across your real apps, under your permissions, with verified results.

### One-line description
Sotto is a voice-first personal operator for Windows: you say what you want in your own words, it does the work across your real email, calendar, files, and apps — and shows you a receipt of what it actually did.

### Product promise
**Whatever you ask of your computer gets done — and Sotto will never tell you it's done when it isn't.**

### Core philosophy
You give intent. It does the computer work. And it shows you the receipt.

### Product principles
1. Intent over instructions. · 2. Do, don't just answer. · 3. Verify, then report. · 4. Ask only when it matters. · 5. Remember, don't re-ask. · 6. Speed is respect. · 7. Calm surface. · 8. Always stoppable.

### Signature capabilities
1. **Verified doing** (the receipt) · 2. **Real-app reach** · 3. **A memory that compounds** · 4. **The brief** · 5. **Routines that are you** · 6. **Conversation that feels like delegation**.

### Main feature categories
Talk · Understand · Act · Email · Calendar · Files · Screen · Apps · Memory · Routines · Briefs (· Web, Messages, Monitoring as the reach grows).

### Personality
A brilliant, calm aide. Warm but economical. Never flatters; always honest; stops instantly; asks once; delivers bad news plainly with a next step. Dry humor, rarely, never during work.

### Brand direction
Quiet power. Light and calm by default. One breathing presence, one-line receipts, no dashboards, no noise. Tagline: **"Consider it done."** The brand's enemies: hype language, chatty assistants, techno-mysticism.

### Why this product deserves to exist
Because the gap is real and enormous: people describe outcomes in one sentence, and computers still demand hours of manual operation — and no existing assistant is both *trusted with* and *actually capable of* real computer work. Sotto exists to move that sentence to done, with proof.

### What makes it different
Everyone else: chat without real control, control without reasoning, or agents without accountability. Sotto: **execution + verification + memory + voice**, in one calm product, on a real PC, with secrets kept local. The receipt is the uncopyable part.

### What the product should feel like
Like relief. You speak; the knot unties; you hear a short, honest confirmation; you get on with your life. It should feel like *having handled it* — not like using software.
